Where this applies

Six things that are true at once when a deployment has stalled.

Most are visible in the usage data. The last two are only visible in the room.

01

Usage peaked at launch

The curve rose through the rollout and has been flat or falling since. Weekly actives look acceptable in aggregate and thin out badly by function and region.

02

Experimentation never became workflow

People use the tool beside the work rather than inside it. The process it was bought to change still runs the way it ran before.

03

No shared definition of value

Every function reports a different measure, none of them tied to an operating result, and the executive sponsor cannot answer what the program returned.

04

Managers are not reinforcing it

The middle layer was trained and then left to absorb the change alongside its existing targets. Nothing in their week requires the new way of working.

05

Adoption is uneven and nobody knows why

One team or site carries the program and the rest are waiting it out. The difference gets explained as culture, when it is usually a difference in process, incentive or manager attention that nobody has mapped.

06

The next decision is more licenses

The proposal on the table expands the platform. Nobody has established whether the constraint is access, capability, workflow, incentive or ownership, so the expansion inherits the same stall.

What gets examined

Six places a stall hides, worked in order.

Adoption failures rarely have one cause, but they usually have one binding constraint. Working these in sequence is what separates the constraint from the symptoms it produces downstream.

Workflow

Where the tool sits relative to the work people are measured on, and what it displaces rather than adds.

Management

Whether the manager layer is equipped and expected to reinforce the change, or merely informed of it.

Governance

Who decides what scales, on what evidence, and how vendor-embedded engineering teams are steered rather than followed.

Incentives

What the current targets, review cycles and recognition actually reward, and whether that is the old behavior.

Skills

Whether the gap is training, confidence, or a role that no longer matches the task the tool has changed.

Ownership and measurement

Whether a named person carries the operating result, and whether the measure in place would show the program working.

The engagement

Ten business days, start to written verdict.

Scoped in writing before it starts, run entirely by Tom, and sized so it fits inside an existing program budget without a new approval cycle.

  • Five to eight stakeholder interviews — the sponsor, the program owner, two or three function leads, and managers from the population that was supposed to change.
  • Evidence review — usage and license data, the program's own reporting, workflow documentation, and whatever the vendor has been measuring.
  • One executive working session — the findings put in front of the people who can act on them, before anything is written up.
  • The written verdict — delivered within the ten days, not weeks after.
The deliverable

The Recovery Verdict

Scale · Repair · Stop

A written decision on each workstream in the program, with the reasoning and the evidence behind it, so it survives being forwarded to someone who was not in the room.

  • The binding constraint, named, with what it is costing.
  • A Scale, Repair or Stop call on each workstream, with the reasoning attached.
  • A 90-day recovery plan, sequenced, with a named owner on each move.
  • The measure that would show it working, and who reports it.
